The floor, and what surrounds it

Six deliverables for a game that is already playable. The rules engine, the objective line, the lighting director and the interaction contract are treated as fixed ground; everything proposed here is built on top of them, in their idiom, using the vocabulary already in the repository.

Wireframes are ASCII by request. Table size is 7 citizens, seed 1000, the default deal: Alice (you), Bo, Chen, Dara, Eze, Fin, Gita.

Deliverable 1

Discussion UI — the floor

The failure mode to design against is a chat panel bolted onto a 3D square. The fix is to stop treating discussion as a message stream and treat it as a phase of the day with a place, a light state and a beginning and end — the same way the vote already is.

The shape

The floor opens after the morning report and again after every enacted tile. It runs three to six beats. One beat is one citizen speaking: 1.5–3s of visible deliberation, then a bubble over their figure, then the floor passes. Bots speak from their real internal suspicion; the player's turn arrives when a bot names them, or once per floor as an open turn.

Nothing here is a rules decision. The engine is never asked a question it does not already answer, and no action taken on the floor changes the deal — it changes what the bots believe, which is the AI's own state.

How an accusation feels directed at you

The lighting director already owns the vocabulary: one warm beam, everyone else silhouetted. An accusation borrows that rig at a quarter of its strength.

  • Light. The speaker's lantern warms. Then — and this is the whole trick — a second, cooler warm rim falls on your figure. Two lit people in a dark square is the grammar of an accusation. Everyone else stays cold. Warm budget unchanged: two small pools, not a lit scene.
  • Camera. A 6% push-in and a small yaw so both of you are in frame, over 400ms, eased. It returns when the beat ends. No cut, ever — cuts break the toy-theatre staging.
  • Body. Your own figure turns to face the speaker without your input. You do not control the turn; you control the answer.
  • Sound. The murmur bed lifts and then drops out under the bubble — the square going quiet is the pressure, not a sting.
  • Text. The objective line, already sacred and already warm-when-you-must-act, becomes Chen names you — answer on the floor. One sentence, same slot, same rule.

Answering without typing

The intent strip is a single row of at most five cards at the bottom centre, bound to 1–5, mirroring the panel convention already taught by panels.js (number keys, kbd hints, Esc). Every card shows the exact line your figure will speak, abbreviated to fit — never a category the game translates behind your back.

Intents that need a target take one more keystroke and no more. The strip swaps in place into a name row; seat order, numbers stable across the whole match, dead citizens struck through and unpickable.

Pressure without a broken promise

You asked for both a timed beat and a match that never times out. Those only conflict if the floor is a rules decision. It is not — so the resolution is a hard line drawn once and held everywhere:

the floor
every rules decision
runs on a ~12s oil line. Running out is an answer: you said nothing, and the square logs that you said nothing.
halts for ever, exactly as today. Nominate, vote, draft, powers: no clock, no default, Esc never answers.

Silence must be a real move for this to be fair, so it is card 5 and it is spoken by absence: your figure stays still, the bubble does not appear, and the log records Alice said nothing. Bots read that. A player who wants no part of the floor at all sits on the bench, which already exists and already has no effect on the rules — sitting mutes your open turns for the session and the strip stops appearing.

Where the words live

Bubbles are world-space and transient by design; the record is the log. Every utterance appends to the column's log under its day number, with the speaker's name in the same weight the engine's own prose uses. That gives a player who was orbiting the camera a way to catch up, and it means the discussion layer needs no second panel.

One boundary to hold at implementation time, in the spirit of the existing tests: an utterance is public output and must reach the screen through view.js like everything else. The bot's reason for saying it never crosses. A speech channel wired to the driver's omniscient stream would be the same class of tell the ambience suite was written to prevent.

Acceptance test

A first-time player, given no instruction, answers a direct accusation within 10 seconds of the bubble appearing — and can afterwards name who accused them and what they said back.

Deliverable 2

HUD evolution — the carved column

The column stays where it is and becomes an object: a carved timber board hung at the edge of the square, with three plates on it. What changes is not the position but the editing — eleven status rows become three, and the rest moves to a key.

What earns permanent space

The test: would a competent player look at it more than once a minute, or would they look at it once a day? Once a minute stays. Once a day moves to the ledger.

Permanent
  • Your role and what you know
  • Reform and Seize tracks
  • Speaker and Deputy
  • The last three things heard
  • The objective line (sacred, unchanged)
Contextual — hold Tab
  • Deck and discard counts
  • Next power on the ladder
  • Term limits this session
  • Chaos track (surfaces itself at 2/3)
  • Full roster with seat numbers
  • The whole log, scrollable

Chaos is the exception that proves the rule: it is irrelevant at 0/3 and the loudest thing on screen at 2/3, so it lives in the ledger and promotes itself onto the column when it reaches two. A number that only matters sometimes should only be there sometimes.

Material rules, so it stays restrained

  • Timber #684828–#583828 is the frame and only the frame. Parchment #d8d4c8 at low opacity is the reading surface. Brass is a hairline edge, never a fill.
  • A UI warm budget. The style bible caps a night frame at 10% warm pixels. The HUD must be inside that budget, not exempt from it: at night the column has exactly one warm element allowed, and the objective line has first claim on it. If the objective is warm, the role seal is not.
  • The column dims with the rig. It is already a pure function of the view; make its opacity one too — one more entry in the thirteen named lighting states, crossfading on the same two-second curve so the UI and the square change together instead of the UI sitting flat over a scene that moved.
  • No new colours. Reform blue, Seize rust, chaos amber and the lantern gold are already measured. The redesign is a material and typography pass, not a palette one.

Held, not toggled — you cannot forget it is open, and it cannot cover the square while a bot is speaking. It goes inert like the HUD does when a decision panel is up, using the machinery already in panels.js. Tab inside a panel keeps its current meaning; the ledger is only reachable when no panel is open, so the two never collide.

Acceptance test

During the night trial state, a screenshot of the frame measures under 10% warm pixels with the HUD included — and a player asked "how many Seizes are down?" answers correctly in under two seconds without opening anything.

Deliverable 3

Juice map — five moments

Ranked by how often the moment happens multiplied by how much it decides. The trial beam exists; four of these five are that rig pointed somewhere new, which is the cheapest kind of polish there is.

1

The accusation aimed at you

many times a match · low effort

Detailed in section 1. It goes first because it is the moment the new feature has to sell, it happens more than any other dramatic beat, and it reuses the beam at quarter strength. Camera 6% push, 400ms ease, returns on beat end. Light speaker's lantern up, cool warm rim on you, crowd cold. Sound murmur lifts then cuts to near-silence under the bubble. Timing 1.5–3s think, 400ms move, then the oil line.

2

The ballot reveal

every day · medium effort

Today the whole election resolves in one engine step and the pace layer holds a beat so the light does not spoil it. Spend that beat properly: ballots land on the lectern one at a time, seat order, 180ms apart — pale card for Aye, rust for Nay — and the running count reads beside them. The style bible's tilt-shift belongs exactly here: shallow depth of field for the four seconds of counting, so the town is momentarily a model on a table. Sound the existing tally under each card, then one of two resolutions: lamps lifting, or a single lantern guttering out. Acceptance: a viewer with the sound off knows the result before the count finishes.

3

The tile enacted — and the town going dark

~11 a match · low effort, high return

The Deputy's figure walks the tile to the board and sets it in a slot: a physical thump, the empty slots still visible beside it. Then the part worth building: every Seize permanently extinguishes one street lantern. By the fifth the square is nearly dark and nobody had to be told the score. It is one line of state read from the view, it costs a light per Seize, and it turns the board into weather. Reform lights nothing new — the town holding steady is the Loyalist win condition, and the absence of reward is the point.

4

The purge

0–2 a match · low effort

The beam narrows onto the named citizen until it is the only light in the square. Total silence for 800ms — the bed cuts, not fades. One gavel. The figure topples by its own depth, which the cast already supports, and the nameplate goes dark and stays hanging, dark, for the rest of the match. Nothing else: no particles, no shake, no red. The restraint is what makes it land, and it is what stops the game reading as arcade.

5

The reveal at game over

once a match · medium effort

The square already settles into the winning team's colour. Build the reveal as a curtain call rather than a table: each figure turns to camera in seat order, 250ms apart, and their role seal presses onto their own nameplate where it stays. The Dictator turns last and is held for 1.2s as the only warm thing in frame. The existing reveal table then appears beneath it for anyone who wants to read it. Acceptance: the player can name the Dictator without reading a word.

Deliberately not on the list: nomination, sitting on the bench, ringing the bell, opening the ledger. They happen constantly and decide nothing — polish there is noise, and the bell was already reported as a chore once.

Three rules keep it from becoming a wall. One concept per day — if a day would teach two, the second waits for tomorrow. Nothing new is explained by a panel that also asks for a decision; the explanation is the footnote line the panels already have. Nothing is modal that was not modal anyway — no tutorial popup exists at all, so there is nothing to dismiss and nothing to skip.

The one addition is a rule for light: during match 1, exactly one object in the square is warm at a time, and it is the object the objective line names. From match 2 the rig goes back to its normal thirteen states. The tutorial is a lighting cue, so it costs no new UI and cannot be left switched on by accident.

Acceptance test

Someone who has never played Secret Hitler completes day 1 — bell, nomination, vote, result — without asking the person next to them a single question, and can then say in their own words what Reform and Seize do.

Deliverable 5

Retention without dark patterns

The honest engine of “one more match” in a deduction game is being shown how wrong you were. Everything below is ranked by that, and nothing below punishes absence, tracks a streak, or spends the player's time to buy something back.

Acceptance test

Of ten playtesters shown the dossier at the end of a loss, at least six start another match without being asked to — and none of them can name a reward they received for doing so.

Deliverable 6

Purged on day 3

In a party game the dead player is bored because they must stay silent among people who are still talking. Alone at a desk, that constraint does not exist — and a single-player game may do the one thing a table cannot: it may tell you the truth early.

One choice, offered once

The moment your figure topples, the trial beam finds you and one panel opens — the last decision the game will ask you for. It asks nothing about rules, and it cannot be reopened.

Open the ledger converts the rest of the match from suspense into dramatic irony, which is the stronger of the two for a spectator. Every nameplate carries its true role from then on; the bubbles stay exactly as they were, and watching Chen calmly claim two Seizes while you can read DICTATOR under his name is better theatre than the match you were playing. Keep your eyes shut preserves the guess for players who want the reveal to land at the end.

And then three small things

  • You get the good seat. Sitting on the bench — which already exists and already does nothing — now moves the camera to a fixed staging view with the tilt-shift permanently on. You are watching a toy theatre, which is what the art direction has been describing all along. Stand up and you are walking the square again.
  • The pace is yours. Bot pace goes to 2× the moment you die, with a line in the objective slot saying so and one key to put it back. The dead should never be made to wait at living speed.
  • Something to be right about. With your eyes shut, the intent strip is replaced by a prediction strip on the same keys: who dies next, whether this government passes, what the next tile is. Scored on the dossier at the end — “you called the Dictator on day 5, two days after they purged you.” With the ledger open you predict outcomes instead of roles, since you already know them.
Acceptance test

A player purged on day 3 is still watching when the match ends, and can say afterwards which citizen they were watching most closely and why.

Before anything is built

Three decisions for the owner

  1. 1

    Is the floor inside the engine or outside it?

    Outside — a presentation layer that reads the view and writes only to the AI's beliefs — keeps the human-driver replay contract, the pace suite and the seeded determinism exactly as they are, and lets the oil line exist. Inside — a real decision kind with options and submit — makes speech replayable and testable like every other action, and makes “say nothing” a recorded move, but every clock in the design then has to go, because the engine has no timeouts by design. This one decision sets the shape of section 1, and it cannot be deferred.

  2. 2

    May the dead see the truth?

    Section 6 assumes yes, as an offered choice. It is a deliberate break with the board game and it means view.js must gain a legitimate, audited path that discloses roles to a dead seat — a change to the file whose whole purpose is that such paths are added on purpose and never by accident. Worth doing, but it is a security-shaped decision, not a UX one.

  3. 3

    How much of the HUD is allowed to become the world?

    Section 2 keeps the column and restyles it, as instructed. But the tracks want to be a real board on the dais, the log wants to be a notice board you walk up to, and the ladder wants to be five brass rungs on the podium. If that is the eventual destination, the column should be built now as a temporary tenant — thin, cheap, easy to delete — rather than as a beautiful piece of carved furniture nobody wants to throw away later.

Nothing in this document changes a rule, touches the seeded stream, or asks the presentation layer to read the game object. Where a proposal needs data it does not have, it says which file would have to disclose it and why that is a decision rather than a task.
