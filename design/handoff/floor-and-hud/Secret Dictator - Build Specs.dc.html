Four specifications, build-ready

One rule governs all four and is worth stating before anything else, because every keyboard mapping in this document depends on it.

The one grammar

Every citizen owns a number for the whole match. It is stamped on their nameplate, it is the key you press to name them, and it is the same number in both cameras.

Numbers are never positional. A council view that numbered seats left-to-right and a walking view that numbered them by proximity would be two games wearing one keyboard. Seat number is a property of the person — it comes from the roster order the engine already keeps, it never changes when someone dies, and dead numbers are retired rather than reused.

Specification 1

The council view

A long lens onto a small ring, from slightly above and slightly behind your own shoulder. The compression is the point: a narrow field of view at distance flattens the square into a tabletop, which is the toy-theatre reading the style bible has been asking for since it was written.

Composition is one number

The ring radius r grows with the table, and every other camera value is derived from it, so a 5-seat frame and a 10-seat frame are the same photograph with more people in it. Nothing is hand-tuned per table size.

r         = 2.60 m + 0.24 m × (seats − 5)        ring radius
dolly     = r × 3.40                             camera distance from ring centre
height    = r × 1.95                             camera height above the square
pitch     = −30°                                 fixed, every table size
fov       = 30° vertical                         the compression (walking mode is 60°)
aim       = ring centre, 1.05 m up               chest height of a standing figure
roll      = 0                                    always
near/far  = 0.5 / 60                             the square is 9 m across; nothing else is in frame
Resolved values — set seats in Tweaks
{{ cameraTable }}

Your seat is at the bottom, always. The ring is rotated so your citizen sits on the near arc, screen-bottom-centre, back three-quarters to camera — you see your own hat and shoulders, which is what makes it your seat rather than a diorama. Everyone else fills the ring clockwise in roster order from your left. The board sits at ring centre on the dais, readable at this pitch without a second camera.

Orbit is constrained, zoom is gone. Drag yaws ±18° and returns to centre on release over 600ms; pitch is locked; the wheel does nothing. The composition is the specification — a free camera would let a player un-frame the game and then report the framing as broken.

Nameplates and marks at this distance

  • Fixed pixel size, never scaled. 88×34px, 13px name, 11px number, regardless of how far the figure is. These are theatre labels pinned above a figurine, not signage inside the world. The existing SOCKET_label per variant still decides the anchor height, so the four cast members keep their own clearances.
  • The number is the left third of the plate, in brass, separated by a hairline. It is what you press.
  • At most three marks, on a strip beneath the name, all brass or dim, never coloured: gavel, deputy, term-limited. Colour on a plate would be a channel available for leaking allegiance; keeping plates monochrome makes the anti-tell test cheap to write.
  • Your ledger's flags appear here as a single amber dot — the one mark that is yours rather than the square's. It means "you have an unread flag on this citizen", nothing more.
  • Dead plates stay. The plate goes dark, the name strikes through, the number is retired and never reassigned. A dead citizen you can still point at is the memory the ledger is built on.

Specification 2

The structured claim schema

Speech is data the engine keeps, so it must obey the same discipline as everything else the engine keeps: nothing in a claim is true, nothing in a claim is private, and every field either is public record or was publicly uttered. A schema with no hidden fields cannot leak, and the leak test becomes a shape check.

The envelope

Utterance {
  id          "u-14"          match-unique, ordinal, assigned by the engine
  day         4
  floor       "f-6"           which floor this was said on
  seq         2               beat index within that floor
  speaker     3               seat number, never a name
  kind        CLAIM_HAND | ACCUSE | SUPPORT | QUESTION | SILENCE
  target      5 | null        seat number, for the kinds that take one
  basis       <enum, see below> | null
  refs        { … }           ids ONLY: government_id, utterance_id, power_id, floor_id
  amends      "u-9" | null    a later claim correcting an earlier one by the same speaker
  text_id     "deny.voted_with_gavel.2"   which authored sentence was spoken
}

FORBIDDEN, at schema level, enforced by an allowlist test:
  role · team · true hand · confidence · suspicion · weight · sincerity
  free text · anything derived from G that view.js would not disclose

text_id rather than a string is what keeps the record small, translatable and diff-able, and it is what lets the same utterance be spoken by a bubble, written into the log, and summarised in the ledger without three copies of the prose drifting apart.

CLAIM_HAND

The only kind that asserts a fact about hidden material, and therefore the only kind that can be caught lying.

CLAIM_HAND {
  refs.government   "g-3"        must be a RESOLVED government
  seat_role         speaker | deputy      must match the public record for g-3
  drawn             {reform:1, seize:2}   speaker only; counts, never an order
  passed            {reform:0, seize:2}   speaker only
  received          {reform:0, seize:2}   deputy only
  blocked           true | false          deputy only, only if the Block was available
  enacted           REFORM | SEIZE | null denormalised from the public record
}

VALIDITY — a claim failing any of these cannot be constructed:
  V1  speaker held that seat in that government (public)
  V2  the government has resolved
  V3  counts sum correctly: drawn = 3, passed = 2, received = 2
  V4  one CLAIM_HAND per speaker per government, unless amends is set
  V5  a dead citizen makes no claims
  V6  the claim is made on a floor that is open to that speaker

Contradiction rules — evidence, never verdicts

Each rule is computable from the public record plus prior utterances alone, which is exactly why a test can enforce it and why showing a flag to the player leaks nothing.

rule
condition
flag emitted
C1
A speaker's passed is not a subset of their drawn.
self · impossible
C2
A deputy's received does not contain the tile the record says was enacted.
record · contradicts the board
C3
Speaker's passed ≠ deputy's received for the same government.
pair · one of the two is lying
C4
Claimed draws across the match exceed the deck's composition, read from the engine rather than hard-coded.
deck · somebody has over-claimed
C5
A later claim about the same government differs from an earlier one and amends is unset.
self · changed story
C6
A citizen accuses on a basis whose refs their own prior claims contradict.
self · argues against their own record

A flag names a rule and its references and stops. It never says who lied, and in the C3 case it cannot: the pair is flagged and the player decides. That restraint is what keeps the deduction in the player's head instead of in a badge.

SUPPORT { target, basis, refs }
  basis: corroborate(utterance) · partner(government) · vote_pattern · claim_consistency
  Valid only for a living target who is not you. Supporting a flagged claim is
  itself referencable later — SUPPORT is how a citizen ties themselves to another.

QUESTION { target, about, refs }
  about: hand(government) · vote(government) · accusation(utterance) · silence(floors[])
  Creates an OBLIGATION: the target takes the first beat of the next floor.
  A QUESTION cannot be aimed at a dead citizen, or at a hand that was never held.

SILENCE { prompted_by, explicit }
  prompted_by: floor_open | question(utterance) | accusation(utterance)
  explicit: true if chosen, false if the beat simply ran out.
  Both are public and they are different events. The distinction is the whole
  social weight of the mechanic, so it is recorded, not inferred.

Acceptance checklist · schema
  • A field allowlist test over every utterance of 50 complete matches passes, and fails when a role field is added on purpose.
  • The permutation test extends to speech: rewrite the roles a seat may not know, and its utterance stream and every ledger entry serialise identically.
  • Every contradiction rule C1–C6 has a fixture that triggers it and a near-miss fixture that does not.
  • No invalid utterance can be constructed: the constructor rejects V1–V6 violations, and a fuzz sweep over random field values produces no accepted invalid claim.
  • Ledger entries rebuilt from a replayed match are byte-identical to the ones built live.
  • A match played with the floor disabled produces the same event log as the same seed with the floor enabled, proving speech changed no rule.
Specification 3

Your turn on the floor

An accusation lands. Everything the player needs is on screen inside one second, and nothing new arrives after that — a first-timer reads for eight seconds and answers; an expert has already pressed the key.

The order of arrival

   0 ms   the murmur bed cuts out — silence is the first signal
   0 ms   accuser's lantern lifts; a cooler warm rim finds YOUR figure
 200 ms   your figure turns to face them (you do not steer this)
 400 ms   camera settles: both of you in frame          [instant if reduced motion]
 400 ms   the bubble is readable above the accuser
 600 ms   objective line swaps: "Chen names you — answer on the floor."
 700 ms   the tray's centre becomes the intent strip (140 ms, ease-out, 44 px rise)
 700 ms   the oil line begins to burn
──────    nothing else appears, ever. If it is not here by 700 ms it is not coming.

The strip is a change of contents in an element already on screen, not a new panel. That is why it can appear this fast without startling: the tray has been at the bottom of the screen all match.

How the intents are chosen

Four to six slots, filled by a pure function of the public record and the utterance that prompted you. A slot is only offered if the schema in section 2 would accept the resulting utterance — so the strip cannot produce an invalid claim, and its contents are testable without a renderer.

When at least one citizen has a basis stronger than gut available. The basis is auto-selected as the strongest the record supports, and shown.

Stable order is what makes the expert fast. Slot 1 is always the answer, the last slot is always silence, and the middle contracts rather than reorders. Two matches in, a player presses 1 before the sentence has finished rendering — which is the two-second answer the acceptance test asks for.

Cards show the sentence, truncated at 34 characters; the highlighted card's full sentence is printed verbatim on the tray's second line. You never speak something you have not read.

The oil line, and turning it off

A 2px brass-gold rule under the tray, burning down over {{ oilLabel }}. It does not flash, pulse, or change colour. The pressure is carried by sound instead: over the last three seconds the crowd murmur fades out, so the square goes quiet around you. Quiet is the game's loudest instrument and it costs no pixels.

Running out is an answer — SILENCE, explicit: false — and the record knows the difference between that and pressing the silence key. Settings → “The floor waits for you” replaces the oil line with a static brass rule and holds the beat indefinitely; silence must then be chosen. Nothing else changes, bots keep their own pace, and no rules decision has ever had a clock in either setting.

What silence costs

Observable consequences only. No meter, no score, nothing invisible:

  • The log records it, by name, in the same prose as everything else: Alice said nothing.
  • The accuser gets a free follow-up beat, appended to this floor. An unanswered accusation is spoken twice.
  • Two silences make you a legal target for basis: silence — an accusation that could not otherwise be constructed.
  • Staying silent after a QUESTION is recorded distinctly and is the most expensive of the three.

And it is genuinely right sometimes: a Rebel who was passed two Seizes and says nothing is playing well. The cost has to be real and bounded, which is why it is four specific consequences and not a hidden number.

Acceptance checklist · the turn
  • A first-time player, uninstructed, answers a direct accusation within 10 seconds — measured from bubble to keypress, over ten testers.
  • A player on their third match answers within 2 seconds at least half the time.
  • Nothing appears on screen later than 700ms after the bubble.
  • Every sentence a card can speak is printed in full somewhere on screen before it is spoken.
  • The strip never offers an intent the schema would reject — fuzzed over 50 matches, zero rejected submissions.
  • With “the floor waits for you” on, a match left alone for ten minutes is exactly where it was, and the log shows no silences.

The rule for what goes in the tray versus a centred card: if the decision requires showing you private material — the three tiles, the two tiles, a Foresight read — it takes a centred card, because private material deserves the middle of the screen and a deliberate close. Everything else lives in the tray. That is four decisions in the tray (nominate, vote, block response, power target) and three on a card (speaker draft, deputy draft, foresight). The tray decisions are the ones that happen every day; the card decisions are the ones that happen behind a closed door.

Pinning pauses the presentation, not the game. Bots stop deliberating and the light holds; the engine is not touched, and the pace suite already proves that stopping and starting the clock cannot change a match. The word “paused” sits in the ledger's header so the state is never ambiguous, and the tray's contextual row is still live behind it — a decision you own can be answered with the ledger open, which is exactly when you want to answer it.

Acceptance checklist · tray, card, ledger
  • Every phase of a complete match has a defined tray state, and none of them is blank — swept headlessly over 50 matches.
  • The private card's pixel dimensions are identical in every state of every match, tiles or no tiles, both cameras.
  • Nothing outside the private card carries role colour, checked by sampling the rendered UI layer for the role palette.
  • Opening the ledger, waiting a minute and closing it produces a byte-identical event log to not opening it.
  • Every ledger row traces to a public utterance or a public record entry; a permutation of unknown roles leaves the ledger identical.
  • The objective line's position, size and behaviour are unchanged from today in both cameras.

Where the two cameras cannot share a grammar

Three places. Forcing any of them would produce a rule that is true on paper and wrong in the hand.

  1. 1 · The objective line's verb

    “Walk to the podium and name a Deputy” is a lie in council view, where there is no walking. The mapping in objective.js is right and must not be duplicated — so keep the id and the named object, and split only the verb clause: one table of two phrasings per id, selected by camera mode. “Walk to the podium and name a Deputy.” / “Name a Deputy — press E, or a number.” The existing objective test then asserts both columns are total and neither leaks. This is the one file the camera decision genuinely reaches into.

  2. 2 · Proximity, facing, and the bench

    The interaction contract — range, a facing cone, which of two overlapping objects wins — has no meaning when you are not in the square. Do not simulate it with a cursor. In council view E opens whatever decision is pending, full stop, and the routing rule survives only as which visual the decision opens against. The bench likewise has no council equivalent: sitting out becomes a tray action (0 — say nothing this session) rather than a place you walk to. Two implementations, one outcome, and the honest version of that is a small branch rather than an abstraction.

  3. 3 · WASD, and the cost of a dead key

    A player who has walked will press W in council view. Doing nothing is bad; repurposing the keys is worse, because the muscle memory returns when they switch back. Proposal: W nudges the camera yaw by 6° and springs back — a movement that acknowledges the press, changes nothing, and cannot be mistaken for walking. If the A/B test settles on council-only, delete it; if both ship, this is the smallest honest answer.
